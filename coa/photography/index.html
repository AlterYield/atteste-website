Certificate of authenticity for a photograph

A photographic print's certificate of authenticity should say how and when the print was made, not only when the picture was taken. Record the print process and paper, the print date, who printed it, the image and sheet sizes, and this print's number within the edition, counting every size. What makes a photography COA different?

A photograph can be printed many times, so the certificate has to describe this one physical print. Collectors look for:

  • Print process. Archival pigment print, gelatin silver print, chromogenic (C-type) print, platinum-palladium print, dye sublimation on aluminium.
  • Paper or substrate, and mounting. For example Hahnemühle Photo Rag 308 gsm; face-mounted to acrylic; dibond backing.
  • Two dates. When the image was made, and when this print was made. A print made years later is a different object from a print made at the time.
  • Who printed it. You, or a named lab or master printer.
  • Image size and sheet size. Borders matter on photographic paper.

How do I number a photographic edition?

Write this print's number and the total, for example "3/10", and state the whole edition across every size in the statement: "Edition of 10 at 50 × 70 cm and 5 at 100 × 140 cm, plus 2 artist's proofs in each size." Buyers value a photograph partly by how many prints can ever exist. If you commit to never printing the image again beyond the edition, say so.

Where should a photograph be signed?

Usually on the back of the print or on the margin, in pencil or archival ink, sometimes with a label. Say where in the statement, and avoid ink that can bleed through the paper.

Which image file should I use for the fingerprint?

Use a reference image of the edition, a photograph of this print or a dedicated JPEG export, rather than your working master file, which you may need to edit later. The fingerprint on the certificate matches only the exact file you choose, so archive that file unchanged.

One certificate per print?

Yes. Each print in the edition gets its own certificate with its own number. The image and fingerprint can be the same across the edition; the edition number is what tells the certificates apart.
