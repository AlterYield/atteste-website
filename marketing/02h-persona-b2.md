### A Day in Michael's Life

- **7:00 AM** — Reads Art Review, Frieze, and local art coverage over coffee. An artist he's been following has a new institutional show. Files this mentally for three clients who might be interested
- **8:30 AM** — Call with a Johannesburg-based client (corporate collection, R25M portfolio). CEO wants a "state of the collection" report for the board meeting. Michael has the information but it's scattered across his files — pulling it together will take two full days
- **10:00 AM** — Studio visit in Woodstock with an emerging sculptor. Taking notes, photographing work, assessing quality and market trajectory. Will he recommend this artist to clients? Possibly. In 18 months, after more exhibitions
- **12:00 PM** — Lunch with a gallerist from Stevenson. Exchanging intelligence: who's buying what, which artists are being courted by international galleries, market sentiment. This is the invisible economy of information that makes Michael valuable
- **2:00 PM** — Client meeting at their Constantia home. Walking the collection with them. They want to deaccession 5 works and acquire 3 new ones. Michael photographs current hang, takes notes on preferences, and promises a proposal within two weeks
- **4:00 PM** — Back in his home office. His "filing system": 14 client folders on his laptop, each containing Word documents, spreadsheets, PDFs, and photos at various stages of organization. His assistant has been trying to standardize this for 6 months
- **5:30 PM** — Prepares a valuation summary for an insurance renewal. Pulls auction comparables from three different websites, cross-references with his own records, and formats everything into a presentable document. This single report takes 3 hours
- **8:00 PM** — Dinner with Melissa (his wife, a landscape architect). She asks how the business is going. "Busy. But I spend more time on paperwork than on actual advisory. I didn't get into this to be an administrator"

### Current Business Operations

**Client management:**
- Each client has a folder on his laptop: collection inventory (Excel), acquisition history (Word), correspondence (email), photos (folders, inconsistently organized)
- No centralized client management system
- Client communication via email and WhatsApp. No CRM. No systematic follow-up tracking
- Some clients have their own systems (or no system at all) — Michael often serves as the de facto registrar

**Collection management (for clients):**
- Maintains inventory spreadsheets for each client. Format varies by client
- Valuations performed annually for major clients, biannually for others. Based on auction comparables and gallery enquiries — all researched manually
- Condition reports written ad hoc — no standardized template or system
- Provenance documentation maintained in paper files with digital backups (inconsistent)
- Photography: mix of professional photos and his own phone photos

**Acquisitions:**
- Research is manual: auction databases, gallery visits, studio visits, art fair scouting
- Presents acquisition proposals as Word documents with images, price, artist CV, and rationale
- No systematic way to show clients options side-by-side or track their preferences over time

**Reporting:**
- Annual collection reports: 20–30 page documents per client, manually compiled
- Include: collection overview, new acquisitions, deaccessions, valuation updates, market commentary, recommendations
- Each report takes 15–25 hours to produce
- Clients love the reports. Michael dreads producing them

**Pain points:**
- **Time on admin vs. advisory:** 40% of his billable time is spent on documentation, reporting, and data management. This should be 10%
- **Scaling is impossible:** At 14 clients he's at capacity — not because of advisory demand, but because of administrative load. He turns down 3–4 potential clients per year
- **No single source of truth:** Client collection data lives in multiple formats across multiple locations. His assistant spends hours reconciling information
- **Valuation research is painful:** Every valuation requires manual research across fragmented SA auction databases. No tool aggregates this for the SA market
- **Reporting is a bottleneck:** The annual reports are his biggest value demonstration to clients — and his biggest time drain. 15–25 hours per client × 14 clients = ~280 hours/year on reports alone
- **Client onboarding is slow:** When a new client engages him, inventorying their collection from scratch takes 2–4 weeks of intensive work
- **Succession risk:** If Michael is incapacitated, his assistant cannot reconstruct his advisory practice. The knowledge is too personalized
- **Professional credibility at scale:** International clients and institutions expect institutional-quality documentation. Michael delivers it, but at enormous personal cost

### Michael's Attesté Journey

**Month 0 — Evaluation**
- Hears about Attesté from a gallery owner who's using it. "They have an advisor module?"

**Month 1 — Pilot (3 Clients)**

**Month 6 — Business Growth**

### The Emotional Core

Michael is not buying software. He is buying:

1. **Leverage** — The ability to serve more clients without proportionally more work. His expertise is the bottleneck; admin shouldn't be
2. **Professional legacy** — A system that captures 25 years of advisory knowledge in a structured, transferable format. If he retires or is incapacitated, the work survives
3. **Client trust** — Enhanced transparency and reporting quality that deepens the trust relationships his business depends on
4. **Revenue growth** — Direct: more clients from freed capacity. Indirect: higher retention from better service quality
5. **Quality of life** — Fewer hours on admin, more hours on the work he loves — studio visits, market analysis, collector conversations. The reason he became an art advisor in the first place

This is a business leverage sale. Attesté multiplies Michael's most scarce resource: his time and expertise.
