### A Day in Sarah's Life

- **7:00 AM** — Coffee. Inbox: 47 emails. A collector in Munich wants to know if the Luyanda Mpahlwa painting is still available. An artist needs an updated CV for a grant application. Strauss & Co wants to confirm loan details for a catalogue raisonné. An intern is sick
- **8:30 AM** — Drives to the gallery in Woodstock. Passes three new galleries that opened this year. Competition is real
- **9:00 AM** — Opens the gallery. Walks the current exhibition. Adjusts one spotlight. Checks the price list — one red dot from yesterday (a R28,000 painting to a Joburg collector). Good
- **10:00 AM** — Call with a London collector who saw the gallery at 1-54. Wants a studio visit next time she's in Cape Town. Sarah takes notes, sends follow-up email with artist portfolio PDFs attached. Manually
- **11:00 AM** — Artist meeting. Reviews new work for the next exhibition. Discusses pricing strategy. The artist wants higher prices; Sarah counsels patience: "Let's build the secondary market first"
- **1:00 PM** — Lunch at her desk. Updates the gallery's inventory spreadsheet. 340 works across 12 artists. Some sold, some on consignment, some in storage, some at collectors' homes, some at art fairs, some at the framers. Tries to reconcile the numbers. Gives up after 30 minutes
- **2:30 PM** — Thandi handles a walk-in. Serious collector from Nigeria. He buys a R45,000 sculpture. Sarah generates an invoice in Word, prints a certificate of authenticity, takes a payment, and makes a mental note to update the inventory. (She'll forget)
- **4:00 PM** — Packaging a work for international shipping. Condition report needed. Writes one by hand. Photographs the work with her phone. Sends to the logistics company
- **5:30 PM** — Starts preparing for Investec Cape Town Art Fair (3 months away). Booth costs, shipping logistics, price lists, promotional materials, travel, accommodation, insurance for works in transit. She's doing all of this in Google Docs, spreadsheets, and email threads
- **7:00 PM** — Home. Still answering emails. A collector asks for provenance on a work she sold 4 years ago. She digs through old invoices. Can't find it immediately. Will look tomorrow
- **9:00 PM** — Reads an article about how a major gallery uses a collection management platform. "Must be nice to have the budget for that"

### Current Gallery Operations

**Inventory management:**
- Google Sheet with 340+ line items: artist, title, medium, dimensions, year, edition size, price, status (available/sold/on loan/consignment), location
- Updated inconsistently (Thandi updates sales; Sarah updates everything else, when she remembers)
- No version control — multiple versions floating in email attachments
- Physical files in the back office: invoices, consignment agreements, shipping documents
- Artist portfolios maintained as PDFs, PowerPoints, and Google Docs — no standardized format

**Sales process:**
- Price list: Word document, reformatted for each fair and exhibition
- Invoices: Word template, manually filled. No automated numbering
- Certificates of authenticity: Word template with gallery letterhead. Individually produced
- Payment tracking: bank statements + manual reconciliation with the spreadsheet
- Collector database: a mix of Mailchimp (for newsletters), Google Contacts, and Sarah's memory

**Documentation:**
- Condition reports: handwritten or typed ad hoc, no standard template
- Photography: mix of professional (for exhibitions) and phone photos (for everything else)
- Provenance: exists in the chain of invoices and certificates but not aggregated. To trace provenance, Sarah has to reconstruct it from paper files
- Exhibition history: maintained per-artist in CV documents, not linked to individual works

**Art fair operations:**
- Separate spreadsheet per fair: works selected, pricing, shipping status, sales at fair, post-fair follow-up
- Insurance: temporary exhibition policies arranged per fair. Paperwork is painful
- Returns: works that don't sell need to be re-integrated into inventory. This step is often delayed, leading to "ghost" inventory

**Pain points:**
- **Time poverty:** Sarah spends 30% of her time on administration that a proper system would handle automatically. This is time not spent with artists, collectors, or selling
- **Inventory chaos:** At any given moment, she cannot say with confidence exactly which works are where, in what condition, and at what price. The spreadsheet is always slightly wrong
- **Revenue leakage:** Delayed invoicing, forgotten follow-ups, lost leads. She estimates she loses R200K–R400K per year in sales that slip through the cracks
- **Provenance gaps:** When secondary market queries come in ("Who bought this? Can you confirm authenticity?"), the answer requires hours of archaeology through paper files
- **Fair preparation:** Each art fair requires 3–4 weeks of administrative preparation. Generating price lists, condition reports, insurance schedules, and shipping manifests — all manually
- **Scaling is impossible:** She wants to represent more artists, do more fairs, reach more international collectors. But the administrative burden is already unsustainable at current scale
- **Professionalism gap:** When international collectors or institutions ask for documentation, the turnaround time is slow and the format inconsistent. This costs credibility and, occasionally, sales
- **Artist relationship strain:** Artists expect regular sales reports, transparent accounting, and career documentation. Sarah delivers this, but it takes enormous effort and is sometimes late
- **No analytics:** She has no easy way to see which artists sell fastest, which price points convert, which fairs generate the best ROI, or which collectors are most active

### The Emotional Core

Sarah is not buying software. She is buying:

1. **Time** — The most precious resource she has. Every hour saved on admin is an hour spent on what she loves and what generates revenue: artists and collectors
2. **Professional credibility** — The ability to operate with the documentation standards of a major gallery, at a mid-tier gallery's budget
3. **Scalability** — The confidence to represent more artists, do more fairs, and reach more markets without the admin burden scaling linearly
4. **Artist relationships** — Transparent, professional reporting that builds trust and retention. Happy artists stay; good documentation proves good partnership
5. **Revenue recovery** — The R200K–R400K in annual lost sales that slip through the cracks of a manual system. Attesté doesn't just save time — it makes money

This is a business efficiency sale with an emotional undercurrent: Sarah became a gallerist to support artists, not to manage spreadsheets. Attesté gives her back the role she signed up for.
